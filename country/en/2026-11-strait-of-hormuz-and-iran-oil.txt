# Strait of Hormuz and Iran: The 2-Day Security Plan and Oil

The two-day security plan that transforms the Tehran administration's military control in the Strait of Hormuz into a diplomatic bargaining chip directly affects the escalating risk premium and freight costs in global oil markets.

## Iran's New Doctrine: Maximum Deterrence and Concurrent Diplomacy

At a time when the risk of regional conflict is rising, Iran has activated its Maximum Deterrence and Concurrent Diplomacy doctrine. Operating under the behind-the-scenes command and control of the new Supreme Leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, and the executive authority granted to President Masoud Pezeshkian, the Tehran administration has directly tied freedom of navigation in the Persian Gulf to the condition of lifting economic sanctions. With the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps raising the readiness level of the armed forces, Secretary of the Supreme National Security Council Mohsen Rezaei maintains Tehran's asymmetric control capacity over the Strait of Hormuz as the strongest card on the table.

## The 2-Day Security Plan: Sanctions Condition and Leaders' Stance

Carrying out the diplomatic leg of the crisis, Minister of Foreign Affairs Abbas Araghchi conducted shuttle diplomacy along the Doha and Beijing axes, presenting the prepared 2-Day Security Plan to his interlocutors. Tehran commits to fully reopening the Strait of Hormuz to international tanker traffic within 48 hours, provided that the Washington administration lifts its port blockades and economic sanctions. In contrast, U.S. President Donald Trump's contradictory statements—made in the shadow of $47.1 billion in war expenditures, implying on the one hand that the hammer could drop while stating on the other that the conflict will end soon—stoke uncertainty in the markets. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's tendency to escalate military tension also keeps the strain high in the equation.

## Bottleneck in the Hormuz Corridor: Freight Spike and Energy Risk

This mutual standoff in the Strait of Hormuz, considered the lifeline of global hydrocarbon trade, drives up safe-passage costs across world energy corridors. Conditioning tanker transit safety pushes the geopolitical risk premium in crude oil prices upward while multiplying insurance and freight rates in maritime shipping. While the Iranian economy, shrinking due to high inflation and commercial restrictions, tries to open a breathing tube through this step, industrial countries procuring oil from the Persian Gulf face a new supply bottleneck.

## The Bill for Global Markets: Industrial Costs and Pump Prices

The bill this picture will present to global production and markets looks quite clear. If this de facto blockage in the energy transit route persists, the spike in transportation costs could increase industrialists' electricity and fuel expenses; this situation may be reflected onto the ultimate consumer as new waves of price hikes at gasoline pumps and supermarket shelves.
